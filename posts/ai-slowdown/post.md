The appeal of slowing AI development rests on an understandable intuition: when the consequences of a technology are uncertain, allowing more time to understand them seems prudent. Yet the value of that time depends on what happens within it. A laboratory may use a longer development cycle to resolve fundamental weaknesses. An attacker may use the same interval to refine an operation. Any serious proposal for restraint must account for both.

This is the question I find most consequential in Dario Amodei’s [_We Must Pace the Frontier_](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier). His proposal for independent evaluators embedded within frontier laboratories deserves strong support. His broader case for pacing raises a further question: under what conditions would slower capability development leave us better able to manage the technology and its risks?

Independent scrutiny provides a necessary foundation for answering it. The organisations developing powerful systems possess much of the information required to assess them, while also having a commercial interest in the outcome of that assessment. No allegation of bad faith is needed to recognise the difficulty. Incentives shape which uncertainties seem tolerable, which evidence seems sufficient, and which delays seem justified. External evaluators need enough access to challenge those judgements and enough independence to publish conclusions that the organisation would prefer to withhold.

Such scrutiny should make our decisions more discriminating. It may establish that a particular system requires further work before release. It may reveal that an apparently reassuring safeguard offers little protection. It should also be capable of finding that delaying a useful defensive capability would leave an avoidable vulnerability unresolved. Independence matters partly because the appropriate conclusion cannot be settled in advance.

My reservation about a general slowdown begins with this last possibility. Restraint among responsible developers does not impose equivalent restraint on malicious actors. Nor does it remove capabilities already in circulation. Even without access to a more advanced model, an attacker could make progress through greater scale, better integration, or more effective use of existing systems. A period of limited frontier development could therefore coexist with a substantial increase in practical danger.

Consider, as a thought experiment, an operation running ten thousand agents. Their number tells us little about their collective intelligence. They might share the same limitations, reproduce the same errors, or encounter a problem none can solve. There is no sound arithmetic by which ten thousand agents, each possessing some fraction of human ability, become an equivalent number of human experts.

For many forms of abuse, however, that is not the relevant comparison. An operation that succeeds only occasionally may still be effective if attempts are inexpensive and sufficiently numerous. Searching for exposed weaknesses, varying fraudulent approaches, and pursuing promising targets are activities in which throughput can matter greatly. The difficulty for defenders arises even when the individual agents remain unimpressive.

Human attention cannot be the compulsory gateway for every response to such activity. Effective defence will require substantial automation, with people determining policy, supervising consequential decisions, and investigating cases that warrant their judgement. A proposal to slow development must therefore consider the defensive capabilities it would defer, alongside the offensive capabilities it might prevent.

This argument has limits. Wider deployment of existing models could improve defensive coverage without a major advance in intelligence. Better engineering could make those models more useful, reliable, and economical. Amodei’s proposal leaves room for continued technical progress, and it would be unfair to treat it as a prohibition on such work. Equally, a more capable model could introduce an attack that existing defences cannot handle. The need for automated protection does not establish that every advance at the frontier improves our position.

What it establishes is the need for a comparative judgement. We must consider how danger develops under both courses of action. Continuing at speed may create new vulnerabilities; delaying may prolong existing ones. Uncertainty attaches to each possibility. It cannot reasonably be treated as an objection to only one of them.

There is, moreover, a substantial engineering case for deliberate restraint. Anyone who has maintained a complex software system will recognise how immediate output can come at the expense of future progress. Fragile infrastructure, unreliable tests, and unresolved architectural problems gradually increase the cost of every subsequent change. A team can remain visibly busy while steadily diminishing its capacity to deliver.

Under those conditions, a period devoted to the foundations can improve the entire trajectory of a project. I would welcome an analogous approach to AI: time spent resolving technical debt, improving training infrastructure, developing dependable evaluations, and strengthening containment. Advances in chips and machine efficiency could also make later development more productive. A longer interval between major capability advances may be entirely justified if it produces more reliable systems and a stronger basis for subsequent work.

The causal connection matters, though. A delay does not itself produce a better processor or repair a deficient evaluation. Those gains require investment and a programme of work; some may be achievable alongside continued capability development. The case for slowing should identify the constraint that additional time would relieve and explain why the proposed intervention is likely to do so.

This gives independent evaluation a more substantive purpose than endorsing a general preference for caution. Evaluators could help establish whether a proposed delay addresses a real bottleneck, whether the work undertaken during it is succeeding, and whether its benefits outweigh the risks of postponement. Their findings would inform decisions for which laboratories and public institutions must remain accountable.

I would therefore support pacing that has a defined purpose, a credible account of how the time will be used, and conditions under which development can proceed. I would apply comparable scrutiny to arguments for acceleration. The objective is a trajectory on which useful capabilities, engineering reliability, and our ability to prevent harm improve together.

The strongest case for slowing down is that it can leave us capable of achieving more, with greater confidence, over the longer term. That is a case worth making carefully. Its success depends on showing that, while others continue to adapt, the time we gain will leave us better prepared for what follows.
